Recently, two of my classes started talking to each other.

In a music perception course (MPC) we saw that the ear's pitch resolution, the *just noticeable difference* (JND), is not a fixed number. It depends on frequency. At the same time, in a Turkish classical music class, our teacher explained how the official notation system, **Arel-Ezgi-Uzdilek (AEU)**, divides the octave into 24 unequal pitches measured in *commas*. Then came the interesting part: in practice, musicians use pitches the system cannot write, and that gap is filled by oral tradition, *meşk*, the way makam music is passed from teacher to student by ear.

That left me with a question in three parts. Which pitches do musicians actually use, and which of them can AEU not write? Is one comma, the smallest step in this world, even audible across the whole frequency range? And how does all of this compare with the way other musical cultures cut the octave?

For me this isn't only academic. When I write a bass line or a microtonal part in a production, I need to know which pitch to tune to, and whether a one-comma difference will still be there once it's down in the low end.

A quick word on *makam* for readers outside Turkish music: a makam is a melodic mode. It is a set of pitches plus rules about which notes to stress, how a melody moves through them and where it comes to rest. The final note is called the *durak*.

## Three ways to cut an octave

Everything below is measured in **cents**: 1200 cents make an octave, 100 cents make a Western semitone. Cents are logarithmic, so equal steps in cents sound like equal steps to the ear. In Hz the same step gets bigger as you go up: 100 cents above 392 Hz is 23 Hz, 100 cents above 784 Hz is 47 Hz.

- **Western 12-tone equal temperament (12-TET)** splits the octave into 12 equal steps of 100 cents.
- **AEU** builds 24 pitches by stacking pure fifths (frequency ratio 3/2). The gaps between neighbouring pitches come in three sizes, 1, 3 or 4 commas, which is about 23.5, 66.8 and 90.2 cents. Pitches have names (Rast, Dügâh, Segâh…) and are counted in commas from Rast.
- **The 53-tone equal division** splits the octave into 53 equal steps. One step is a *Holdrian comma*: 1200/53 ≈ 22.64 cents. The idea goes back to Nicholas Mercator and William Holder in the 17th century (not to Pythagoras, as you sometimes read). Turkish theory uses this grid to count commas.

In all figures, one octave runs from Sol (G4) = 392 Hz to 784 Hz, and Rast, the Turkish reference pitch, is placed on Sol.

## Finding 1: AEU sits on the 53-comma grid

AEU is built from pure fifths, the 53-grid from equal steps, so strictly they are two different systems. In practice the difference doesn't matter: every one of the 24 AEU pitches lies within 0.9 cents of a 53-grid line (the largest gap is Dik Bûselik, 0.88 cents). That is far below any JND, so I treat AEU as 24 of the 53 points from here on.

Against the piano the picture is different. Apart from Rast itself, which is defined as Sol, no AEU pitch equals a 12-TET note. The white keys all have an AEU pitch within 8 cents. Three of the black keys, G♯, C♯ and F♯, fall between two AEU pitches about 10 to 14 cents away on either side; A♯ and D♯ have one AEU pitch 6 to 8 cents away.

## Finding 2: practice uses 37 pitches, not 24

To see what musicians actually play, I used **SymbTr** (Karaosmanoğlu 2012), a corpus of Turkish makam scores built for music information retrieval. The current GitHub version has 2,200 pieces in 155 makams, 849,403 notes in total. Every note has two values: its AEU spelling (*KomaAE*) and the pitch an expert says it is really played at, on the 53-grid (*Koma53*).

7.7% of all notes are performed off the AEU grid, and almost every one of those shifts is exactly one comma. I counted a position as *practical* when it is used in at least 10% of some makam's pieces (and in at least 3 pieces). That gives 37 positions: 22 of the 24 AEU pitches plus 15 that AEU can't write.

Going the other way, two AEU pitches are practically unused: Dik Hicaz (comma 30, 81 notes in the whole corpus) and Dik Mâhur (comma 52, only 3 notes). Three grid positions, 20, 32 and 33, never occur at all.

The important point: **the gap is not a transposition artefact.** The *same written note* is played at different heights in different makams. Written Segâh is played at comma 17 in Rast (98% of those notes) and in Segâh makam (99%), but at comma 16, one comma lower, in Uşşak, Bayati, Hüseynî and Saba (about 88%). Hüzzam plays written Eviç one comma lower (86%), and Saba plays written Hicaz one comma higher (91%). The score is the same; the pitch depends on the makam, and on the tradition that tells you which one to play.

This also lines up with an observation in the SymbTr paper: four of the five Turkish folk music (THM) pitches that AEU lacks, B♭2, F♯3, E♭2 and C♯3, are among the most used off-grid pitches in the art music corpus too.

## Finding 3: is one comma audible?

One comma is a fixed ratio, about 1.32% of the frequency (Δf = 0.013164 · f). The JND is not: in Hz it is roughly flat in the bass and grows with frequency. So the question is where the two lines cross.

| Frequency | 1 comma | JND (slide 11) | JND (slide 12) | comma ÷ JND (11 / 12) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Hz | 1.3 Hz | 49.5 cents | 9.2 cents | 0.5 / 2.5 |
| 250 Hz | 3.3 Hz | 20.7 cents | 5.2 cents | 1.1 / 4.3 |
| 500 Hz | 6.6 Hz | 12.8 cents | 3.9 cents | 1.8 / 5.9 |
| 1 kHz | 13.2 Hz | 9.5 cents | 3.4 cents | 2.4 / 6.7 |
| 2 kHz | 26.3 Hz | 8.6 cents | 3.7 cents | 2.6 / 6.1 |
| 4 kHz | 52.7 Hz | 9.6 cents | 5.6 cents | 2.4 / 4.1 |

<p class="note">Slide 11 is the JND curve from our course slides (read off the plot, approximate). Slide 12 is the more sensitive fit by Wier, Jesteadt and Green (1977): log₁₀(Δf) = 0.026·√f − 0.533.</p>

Between 500 Hz and 4 kHz, one comma is about 2 to 7 times the JND, depending on which data you trust. That is comfortably audible, at least for a trained ear in a quiet room. In the bass the margin disappears: with the course-slide values, one comma reaches the JND at about 225 Hz and drops under it below that. That's G3 and everything under it, the whole range of a bass line. The Wier data are more forgiving, but even there the margin shrinks from about 6× to 2.5× at 100 Hz.

And these are best-case numbers measured with steady tones. Short notes raise the threshold further, which matters a lot for fast bass lines (more on that in [my next post, about bass, note duration and pitch detection](/blog/low-pitch-duration)).

## How other cultures cut the octave

Finally, I wanted to see Turkish practice next to other systems. The figure below shows only the *possible* notes of each system, not scales, all started from Sol = 392 Hz so they line up.

<WorldTunings>
  Slendro, pelog and Thai tunings are idealized or averaged; real instruments vary. Arabic and Persian theory has used 24 quarter tones since the 1932 Cairo Congress (and Vaziri), but practice uses neutral intervals that deviate from them. The Chinese chain of fifths doesn't close: Zhonglü ends up about 24 cents above a pure fourth. The śruti list is one common just-intonation version; sources differ.
</WorldTunings>

A few things jump out. The Chinese twelve *lü* and AEU are built the same way, by stacking pure fifths, so their shared pitches line up exactly (Dalü is Zirgüle, Taicu is Dügâh). The Indian *śruti* list contains steps of 81/80, about 21.5 cents, which is close to one Holdrian comma, so the two finest systems in the figure work at roughly the same resolution. And the Javanese gamelan tunings and the Thai scale don't sit on the Western grid at all: they are a different way of dividing the octave, not a slightly detuned version of ours.

## Limitations

- SymbTr's 53-grid pitches are expert annotations of practice, not acoustic measurements, and they can't show differences smaller than one comma.
- Nişâburek seems to be notated a whole tone higher in SymbTr (all its pieces end on Dügâh), so positions 3 and 25 may be transposition artefacts.
- The 10% criterion is a choice. The JND values were read from slide images. Slendro, pelog and Thai tunings vary between instruments.
